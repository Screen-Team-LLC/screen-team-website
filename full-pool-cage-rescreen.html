When a full rescreen beats a partial fix

A full pool cage rescreen is the right call when mesh age is uniform: gray, brittle roof panels, walls that crackle under finger pressure, and doors that no longer seal because the surrounding fabric has lost tension. Spot-replacing two storm tears on a cage that is eight years old often leaves bright new squares next to oxidized neighbors — and the next summer storm finds the next weak row. Chris walks the enclosure and tells you whether a complete refresh or a targeted panel set is the honest answer.

Full jobs replace mesh and spline across walls, roof horizontals, and typically the cage door leaf. The aluminum frame stays. That distinction matters for Tampa Bay budgets: you keep a permitted structure that still stands square, and you avoid the cost and timeline of a structural rebuild.

What Chris includes on a complete cage

  • Removal of all worn screening and old spline from accessible bays
  • Visual check of posts, headers, kick plates, and door alignment before new mesh goes up
  • New fiberglass (or upgraded) mesh tensioned panel by panel
  • Door leaf rescreen and latch check so the cage closes clean
  • Debris cleanup around the deck and pool apron
Full-cage tip: Photograph the roof from inside looking up after a rain. Standing water on slack panels is a countdown clock — weight accelerates the next tear across the whole roof line.

Mesh choices for Florida sun and bugs

Most full cage rescreens use standard fiberglass for balanced visibility and airflow. Homeowners with dogs often upgrade lower walls and the door to pet-resistant mesh. Waterfront Clearwater and Tarpon Springs properties sometimes choose no-see-um screening where smaller insects are the complaint. Aluminum mesh is available when durability outranks view.
