Two-Story Pool Cage Rescreen

Tall cages fail differently — roof bays catch more UV and wind, and access changes the day. Chris Westcott rescreens two-story and high-wall pool enclosures with height factored into the quote from the first conversation.

Why tall cages need a different plan

A two-story pool cage is not just a single-story job stretched vertically. Upper roof panels take harder sun, storm gusts hit higher walls with more leverage, and ladder or scaffold setup affects how long the crew can safely work a bay. Homeowners in East Lake, Carrollwood, and parts of Clearwater and Palm Harbor often discover that a “cheap per panel” quote ignored height — until the installer arrives and the price jumps.

Chris prices tall work honestly. Photos that show the second story, roof pitch relative to the house, and any tight side yards help him plan access before he arrives. You hear about staging needs up front, not as a surprise add-on.

What we evaluate before climbing

  • Wall height and whether a full or partial upper-bay rescreen is needed
  • Ground conditions for ladder footing around the pool deck
  • Neighbor fences, power lines, and narrow setbacks that change approach
  • Door and lower-wall condition while we are already on site
  • Whether full cage scope or storm-row only makes sense
Tall-cage tip: From the second-floor window looking down onto the cage roof, look for waves or puddles in the mesh. Slack upper panels are the ones that unzip first in a summer storm.

Partial upper rows vs complete tall rescreens

Sometimes only the top horizontals failed after a storm while lower walls still hold tension. Chris can replace those upper bays when the rest of the mesh still has life — and he will warn you if color mismatch will be obvious. When the entire tall cage is oxidized, a complete rescreen avoids climbing twice in two seasons.
